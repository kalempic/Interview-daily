Behavioral questions ask for a past event so the interviewer can see how you work. STAR is a way to keep the answer specific: situation, task, action, result. It is not a script that makes a vague answer sound finished.

## Situation and your responsibility

Set a specific situation and your own responsibility. The situation is the constraint, in one or two sentences: what was being decided, and what made it hard. The task is the part that was yours. "We needed to ship" is not a task. "I owned the client approach and had to recommend one" is a task.

Skip the company history. A interviewer cannot use a private codename, an internal tool name, or a confidential customer. Describe the kind of project in words anyone in the interview can follow.

## Actions, including how you listened

Describe your actions, including how you heard the other person. "I disagreed and we discussed it" hides the work. A stronger sequence is concrete: you read their proposal, you restated their goal to check that you understood it, you named a specific risk or offered an alternative, and you agreed on a decision rule.

A decision rule is something you can both look at. A small spike, a written tradeoff, or a metric you will watch for a week is a decision rule. "I am more senior" is not. The listening step matters because the question is about a disagreement, and an answer that never shows you understood the other side sounds like a lecture.

Use "I" for the actions you took. Use "we" for the result you shared. If every sentence is "we", the interviewer cannot tell what you did.

## The result, and what you would repeat

End with a concrete result and what you would repeat. The result is what shipped, what changed, or what you learned and then applied. It does not have to be a victory over the other person. "We kept their approach and added a limit I was worried about" is a real result. "I was right" is not.

Say what you would repeat next time, in one sentence. That shows you can extract a practice from the story. If the outcome was messy, say so. A clean lesson from a mixed result is more credible than a perfect story.

## What to leave out

Stay specific and do not invent metrics or private details. Do not create a percentage, a dollar figure, a team size, or a title to make the story sound larger. If you do not remember the number, describe the direction of the change instead of guessing. Invented precision is easy to trip over in a follow-up question.

Do not name a real employer, a real customer, or a private incident the interviewer has no right to hear. You can say "a previous team" and describe the technical shape of the disagreement. You do not owe a confession, and you do not get credit for gossip.

## A skeleton you can reuse

This skeleton is a pattern, not a memory of a real person.

Situation: two approaches were on the table, and the team had one constraint that made the choice matter. Task: you were responsible for a recommendation, not for winning. Action: you read the other proposal, restated their goal, named one risk, and suggested a spike or a metric as the decision rule. Result: you say what you shipped and one practice you would repeat. You add no metric you do not actually have.

If you cannot fill the action with something you did, you do not have the story yet. Pick another disagreement rather than decorating this one.
